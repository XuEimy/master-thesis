Abstract
The present paper explores how large language models (LLMs) can function as
personal memory systems. Unlike conventional lifelogging technologies, which
passively record data, LLMs can actively participate in memory processes through
dialog, interpretation, and narrative generation. LLMs, we suggest, mark a new
chapter in the history of memory technology, one characterized by the active so-
licitation of mnemonically-relevant information and the co-production of narrative
artifacts. Courtesy of their conversational capabilities, LLMs are poised to influence
memory in the manner of a human social companion. This, we suggest, blurs the
distinction between technologically- and socially-situated approaches to the shaping
of human memory.

1 Introduction

From the earliest knots in strings and marks on clay tablets to the invention of writing
and the printing press, human beings have long relied on external resources to shape
and augment memory (Clowes 2017; Heersmink in press). These material supports
have historically functioned as scaffolds for individual and collective recall, enabling
the preservation and transmission of knowledge across generations. More recent
developments—including photographs, phonographs, digital computers, smart-
phones, search engines, and cloud-based storage—have significantly expanded the
fidelity, flexibility, and immediacy of memory storage, providing new opportunities
for mnemonic augmentation (Clowes 2013, 2015). Lifelogging technologies have
emerged as a particularly important focus area for memory-related research (Dingler
et al. 2021; Harvey et al. 2016). Such devices are designed to record and archive
the fine-grained details of daily life, ranging from conversations and movements to
visual and auditory experiences. As noted by Kalnikaite et al. (2010), these technolo-
gies offer a compelling vision of “prosthetic memory”: a comprehensive, searchable
archive of lived experience, capable of supplementing or even supplanting biologi-
cal recall. They also provide important opportunities for therapeutic interventions
designed to tackle memory impairments caused by injury or disease (e.g., Dubourg
et al. 2016).
   In this paper, we suggest that large language models (LLMs) mark a new chapter
in the history of technologically-augmented memory. Whereas lifelogging systems
primarily function as passive repositories that capture and store information about
lived experience, LLMs offer a more active and participatory mode of memory sup-
port. Courtesy of their dialogic capabilities, LLMs can act as conversational partners,
engaging users in dynamic exchanges that contribute to the formation, retrieval, and
enrichment of mnemonic records. In addition, the generative capabilities of LLMs
support the co-creation of personalized narratives, helping to weave the elements of
lived experience into an evolving life story.
   Our aim in the present paper is to explore the status of LLMs as memory technolo-
gies. In Sect. 2, we explore some of the innovations that enable LLMs to function as
personal memory systems. In particular, we focus on the innovations that are driv-
ing the development of real-world memory systems, such as MemoryBank (Zhong
et al. 2024), Memoro (Zulfikar et al. 2024), and OS-1 (Xu et al. 2024). Section 3
explores the ways that LLMs can contribute to memory processes, focusing on their
role in encoding and retrieval processes. Section 4 briefly considers the theoretical
significance of these developments, focusing on the implications for extended and
embedded approaches to human memory. Section 5 turns to the question of a ‘good’
memory, discussing LLM hallucinations and their relevance to personal memory.
We conclude (in Sect. 6) by highlighting some of the ways that LLMs mark a new
direction for philosophical and empirical research into technologically-augmented
memory.

2 Large Language Models and Personal Memory

LLMs are AI systems that specialize in language-related tasks, such as text genera-
tion, translation, summarization, and question-answering. As with many contempo-
rary AI systems, LLMs are driven by deep (multi-layer) neural networks that are
trained on large datasets. Notable examples of LLMs include OpenAI’s GPT-5,
Meta’s LLaMA, and Google’s Gemini system. These models exhibit exceptional
levels of performance across a wide range of natural language processing tasks.
From summarization and translation to creative writing and dialogue systems, LLMs
are reshaping how machines understand and generate human-like language, often
achieving results that rival or surpass human performance on standard benchmarks
(e.g., Shultz et al. 2025).
    Recent work has explored the intersection between LLMs and human memory
from at least two perspectives. One strand focuses on the relationship between
human bio-memory and memory-related mechanisms in LLM architectures. Dong
et al. (in press), for example, review attempts to treat Memory-Augmented LLMs
(MA-LLMs) as computational models of human episodic memory, evaluating how
well such systems capture properties such as event segmentation, selective encoding,
temporal contiguity, and dynamic updating. A second strand of research focuses on
the use of LLMs to support human memory, either through the development of mne-
monic aids (e.g., Maniar et al. 2025) or (more commonly) through the development
of personalized conversational assistants (e.g., Xu et al. 2024). In principle, there is
no reason why these two strands of research cannot overlap. Indeed, some attempts
to support human memory explicitly rely on the integration of human-like memory
mechanisms. The MemoryBank system, for example, incorporates a memory updat-
ing mechanism inspired by the Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve (Zhong et al. 2024).
Notwithstanding these overlaps, our focus in the present paper relates to the second
strand of research: the use of LLMs to support personal memory.
    To clarify our use of the term, we understand personal memory to be a form of
memory that concerns the encoding, organization, and retrieval of personal data—
information about one’s own experiences, relationships, activities, and life events. It
encompasses emotionally rich, self-referential episodes; semantic information about
one’s past; and the narrative structures that integrate this information into a coherent
sense of identity and temporal continuity. In this broad sense, biological memory
already qualifies as a form of personal memory—it is, after all, a form of memory that
centers on an individual’s personal past.
    The primary challenge for LLM-based personal memory systems is to integrate
personal data into an LLM’s responses—to somehow tailor the model’s activity to
reflect previously encoded information. One way to achieve this is to rely on standard
machine-learning techniques, such as fine-tuning. In practice, however, this approach
is fraught with difficulties. The parameters of most LLMs are frozen once their ini-
tial training is complete, meaning that their internal weights cannot be modified at
inference time. And even when fine-tuning is technically feasible, it is often imprac-

tical due to the risk of catastrophic forgetting1 and the overheads associated with
data preparation. Additional problems relate to issues of transparency, control, and
mnemonic auditing: once information has been absorbed into a model’s parameter
space, it cannot be selectively inspected, edited, or removed. These limitations make
conventional fine-tuning techniques an awkward fit for systems that must remain
flexible, transparent, and responsive to ongoing changes in an individual’s personal
history.
    How, then, can LLMs act as a technological support for personal memory? As
we shall see, the answer comes in the form of a distinction between what is called
parametric (or internal) and non-parametric (or external) memory. In essence, LLMs
are able to interact with external bodies of information (e.g., a repository of person-
specific information), factoring this information into their generative routines. The
distinction between parametric and non-parametric memory serves as the basis for a
further distinction between what we will call parametric and non-parametric learn-
ing. While parametric learning involves adjustments to an LLM’s internal parameters
(i.e., weight matrices), non-parametric learning involves adjustments to the content
of an external memory store. Both types of adjustment are apt to change an LLM’s
responses, but they do so via different means.
    Two additional innovations are relevant to our understanding of LLMs as per-
sonal memory systems. The first is a capacity for multimodal data processing. This
is important when it comes to dealing with the sort of data delivered by conven-
tional lifelogging devices. Wearable cameras, for example, deliver information in
the form of images, while health-tracking devices (e.g., Fitbits) collect information
about physiological, behavioral, and environmental variables. Typically, this infor-
mation needs to be transformed into something that is more amenable to linguistic
processing (e.g., a textual description of an image). The advent of multimodal LLMs
(e.g., GPT-4V) helps to meet this challenge, enabling disparate lifelogging data to be
converted to a common textual format.
    Another important innovation relates to tool use. This refers to the capacity of
LLMs to interoperate with a wider ecology of computational systems (including other
AI systems). This is important because LLMs are mostly intended for language pro-
cessing, and, more specifically, language generation. Accordingly, they sometimes
perform poorly on tasks that require mathematical calculations or logico-deductive
reasoning (see Floridi and Chiriatti 2020). An ability to interoperate with external
tools or systems helps to address this problem, enabling LLMs to integrate external
computational tools into their own generative routines.
    We thus have three innovations that are relevant to understanding the status of
LLMs as personal memory technologies: multimodal capabilities, external memory
and tool use. The remainder of this section offers a brief overview of these innovations.

2.1 Multimodal Capabilities

While early LLMs were constrained to purely textual inputs and outputs, recent
advances have produced a new generation of systems capable of processing and gen-
erating data across a range of modalities, including images, audio, video, and sensor
streams. These multimodal LLMs extend the interpretative and generative reach of
language models, supporting tasks such as image captioning, visual question answer-
ing, and cross-modal reasoning.
   In talking of multimodal LLMs, we should make a distinction between LLMs that
deal with multimodal data via a single architecture (e.g., a transformer architecture)
and those that rely on interactions with other generative AI systems, such as varia-
tional encoders and diffusion models. The former are what might be called unified
multimodal LLMs, while the latter are what might be called modular multimodal
LLMs. One example of a unified multimodal LLM is OpenAI’s GPT-4V model. This
model integrates visual processing into the LLM’s transformer architecture, allow-
ing the model to produce a textual description of the entities depicted in an image.
One example of a modular multimodal LLM comes in the form of LLaVA (Liu et al.
2023), which combines an LLM with a specialized machine vision module.
   In addition to the distinction between unified and modular multimodal LLMs, there
is a further distinction between systems that accept multimodal inputs and those that
produce or generate multimodal outputs. The aforementioned GPT-4V model accepts
text and images as input and generates text as an output. The appeal to multimodality
thus occurs in respect of the model’s inputs. Other LLMs, however, can produce non-
text content (such as images, audio, and videos) as outputs. For example, an LLM
might generate an image based on a textual description. In most cases, these systems
rely on additional generative AI systems to produce the actual content. OpenAI’s
DALL-E (version 2), for example, relies on a transformer architecture for text encod-
ing and a diffusion model for image generation.
   Multimodal capabilities are an ongoing focus of contemporary research efforts,
with newer systems exhibiting increasingly sophisticated abilities. Google’s Veo sys-
tem, for example, is a state-of-the-art video generation model that accepts a combi-
nation of image and text prompts (as input) and produces realistic video and audio
data (as output).2 At first sight, this sort of capability might seem irrelevant to the
status of LLMs as personal memory systems. After all, it is natural to think that the
main objective for an LLM-based personal memory system would be the generation
of linguistic outputs. As we will see, however, the importance of LLMs goes beyond
their ability to transform multimodal data into a natural language format. In addition
to such capabilities, LLMs can participate in the generation of narrative artifacts
with multimedia content, similar to the multimedia biographies that have featured in
dementia-related research (see Smith et al. 2009).

2.2 External Memory

To function as a personal memory system, an LLM needs some way to store personal
data and coordinate its responses with respect to that data. In short, LLMs require a
capacity to assimilate new data into their generative routines. As noted above, such
forms of assimilation can occur via the adjustment of two sorts of memory: paramet-
ric (or internal) memory and non-parametric (or external) memory. Internal mem-
ory refers to the information stored within the model’s internal parameters (i.e., the
weights of a deep neural network, specifically a transformer architecture). This sort
of memory is acquired as a result of the model’s training regime. If, for example, a
model has been trained on a dataset that includes information about the Museum of
Modern Art (MoMA), then the model will be in a position to answer questions about
MoMA once the training is complete. If, for example, the model is asked about the
address of MoMA, the model may respond by saying that “MoMA is on 53rd Street.”
    The problem with internal memory is that it is both fallible (the model may fail to
recall certain information) and difficult to update. The contents of internal memory
are encoded in a highly distributed fashion, possibly across billions of parameters,
and such distributed forms of encoding make it difficult to update specific items of
information. If, for example, the address of MoMA should change, the only option is
to retrain the model on the new data. In general, distributed forms of encoding make
it difficult (if not impossible) to make targeted updates to specific information items
(e.g., those pertaining to MoMA).
    Enter the notion of external memory. External memory refers to a data repository
(typically a vector database) that lies external to the LLM. When the LLM responds
to a user query, it can access this data repository, relying on a retrieval loop to return
query-relevant information. The retrieved information can then be used to tailor (or
‘condition’) the LLM’s response.
    As a means of making this clearer, suppose that an LLM was trained on data that
did not include any information about MoMA. When this LLM is presented with a
question such as “Where is MoMA located?”, the model may respond by saying that
it doesn’t know, or it may report an incorrect response. Now suppose that we cre-
ate a repository of information about MoMA, using information extracted from the
museum’s website. This repository serves as the LLM’s external memory. When the
aforementioned query is presented to the LLM, the LLM retrieves information from
the repository using the query as a ‘retrieval cue’. The execution of this retrieval
action then returns information to the LLM, where it is used as context for the LLM’s
output. The upshot is that the LLM’s response is now coordinated with respect to two
items of information: 1) the user’s original query, and 2) the information retrieved
from external memory. Note that the latter type of information (the information
retrieved from external memory) could be text that specifies the location of MoMA
(e.g., “The main entrance is on 53rd Street, close to Sixth Avenue and just east of our
flagship Museum Store”). In response to this enriched context, the LLM is now able
to issue the correct response to the user’s query: “MoMA is located on 53rd Street.”
The model is able to produce the correct answer, because (in this case) the answer to
the user’s query is already contained in the context that drives the model’s response.
One way to think about this is to regard the external memory as a form of long-term

memory, albeit one that trades in symbolic, linguaform encodings of domain-rele-
vant information. The call to external memory then functions as a retrieval loop that
returns information relevant to the current generative context. The combination of the
user query and the retrieved information then acts as a sort of ‘working memory’ that
drives the LLM’s responses.
   This approach to incorporating external information into an LLM’s generative
routines is known as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) (Lewis et al. 2020;
Gao et al. 2024). The specific details of this technique need not concern us here.3
What matters is simply the idea that an LLM can access external bodies of informa-
tion and factor this information into its generative routines. This capability is central
to understanding how LLMs can function as personal memory systems. While the
parameters of a production-level LLM,4 such as ChatGPT, might be fixed, the con-
tents of external memory are not so constrained. As new information is acquired, it
can be added to external memory, thereby allowing LLM responses to be conditioned
on the newly acquired information. Suppose, for the sake of example, that you are
equipped with a wearable camera that periodically takes a picture of your field of
view. This image can be posted to a multimodal LLM that generates a textual descrip-
tion of the image, and the textual description can then be added to external memory.
If one of the images depicts you engaged in a certain activity (a canoeing activity,
for example), then information about this activity will now be stored as a record in
external memory. If you subsequently ask the LLM whether you have been engaged
in a canoeing activity, the LLM will use your query as a retrieval cue, attempting
to retrieve relevant information from its external memory. Assuming the retrieval
routine succeeds, information about your canoeing trip will be made available to the
LLM, and the LLM will generate its response on that basis.5
   This, in outline form, is how an LLM can be made to work as a personal mem-
ory system without the need for conventional (i.e., parametric) forms of machine
learning. Indeed, the learning that occurs in RAG contexts is via the modification
of external memory, not the adjustment of the LLM’s internal memory. This is what
we earlier referred to as non-parametric learning. A key feature of non-parametric
learning is that it allows a single production-level LLM (e.g., ChatGPT) to function
as the core of multiple personal memory systems, with person-specific data stores
providing the means by which an LLM’s responses are tailored to an individual’s per-
sonal history. This is how the majority of experimental LLM-based personal memory
systems (e.g., MemoryBank, OS-1, and Memoro) are implemented. It is also the
approach adopted by a number of production-level LLMs that rely on a rudimentary
form of personal memory to tailor responses to particular users. In the case of Chat-
GPT, for example, user accounts are associated with a set of “Saved Memories” that

2.3 Tool Use

While RAG represents a powerful technique for extending the capabilities of LLMs,
it is increasingly being generalized into a broader class of what are called aug-
mented LLMs (Mialon et al. 2023). The distinctive feature of RAG is the inclusion
of a retrieval loop that targets a body of domain- or person-specific information,
typically in the form of a vector database. In principle, however, there is no reason
why the retrieval loop could not be directed at other types of resources. Nor is there
any reason to assume that every type of interaction between an LLM and its wider
environment is best understood as a form of retrieval action. At a general level, we
can characterize RAG as the instantiation of an information processing circuit (or
information processing loop) that extends beyond the borders of the LLM. In the
case of RAG, the LLM uses (and sometimes generates) content that functions as a
retrieval cue. In other cases, however, the LLM might generate content that enables
it to invoke an external tool, such as an online calculator or logic reasoner. This is the
basic vision behind the notion of augmented LLMs. It is a vision in which the capa-
bilities of LLMs are augmented courtesy of their interaction with external resources
or tools. By directing attention to the nature of these interactions, we are able to dis-
tinguish different types of augmentation. RAG is, of course, one example, but other
forms of augmentation have been the focus of recent research efforts. These include
the following:

● Knowledge-Augmented Generation (KAG). This form of augmentation close-
  ly resembles RAG, except the external memory consists of a graph database con-
  taining structured knowledge, typically in the form of (computational) ontologies
  (see Pan et al. 2024). The specific virtue of ontologies lies in their capacity to
  support symbolic reasoning and the retrieval of information that respects cer-
  tain semantic constraints. In this sense, KAG is an ideal candidate for semantic
  memory applications.
● Search-Augmented Generation (SAG). SAG integrates real-time search capa-
  bilities into the generative process, typically via an online search engine, such as
  Google Search. This technique has become something of a standard feature of
  production LLMs, such as ChatGPT and Gemini. When prompted for news on
  current affairs, for example, ChatGPT issues a call to a search tool and factors
  the results into its response. In a personal memory context, this capability can be
  used to enrich the information obtained from various recording devices. If, for
  example, a GPS device indicates that an individual is currently in the vicinity of

● Tool-Augmented Generation (TAG). TAG refers to situations where LLMs in-

  to be useful when it comes to the process of emplotment, which refers to the tem-

● Code-Augmented Generation (CAG). Some LLMs can generate executable
  code to perform operations that are too complex to be handled by language pro-
  cessing alone (e.g., Gao et al. 2023). Consider a case where an LLM is tasked
  with computing the total number of steps performed by an individual within a
  given time period. This requires the LLM to engage in a process that it may not be
  optimized for (i.e., a task involving numerical reasoning). One possibility is to re-
  sort to an external tool, such as a calculator. But another strategy is to generate the
  (programmatic) code that implements the desired operation. This code can then
  be executed by a code interpreter and the result returned to the LLM. The total
  number of steps can then be added to external memory. In some ways, of course,
  CAG is similar to TAG, in the sense that the code interpreter might be seen as an
  external tool. On the other hand, CAG introduces us to the idea of LLMs as the
  creators of tools. By generating the code to perform a particular task, the LLM is,
  in effect, creating an ad hoc computational tool—one that is tailored to the task at
  hand and (usually) abandoned once the task is complete.

These different forms of augmentation tend to complicate the rather simplistic image
of LLMs as the sole technological ingredient of a personal memory system. This is
because such systems are likely to involve a multiplicity of technological and infor-
mational resources. In addition, there is no reason to assume that personal memory
systems will be built around a single LLM. In practice, a personal memory system
is likely to involve multiple LLMs, with different LLMs being invoked to perform
different tasks. A multimodal LLM, for example, might be tasked with the processing
of physiological data (see Fang et al. 2024), while another LLM might be tasked with
the optimization of retrieval cues (see Ma et al. 2023). Other LLMs may specialize
in the generation of narrative artifacts, such as textual descriptions of daily events or
significant life periods.
    As noted by an anonymous reviewer, the use of augmented generation techniques
in combination with multi-LLM solutions complicates the implementation of LLM-
based memory systems. In particular, it raises questions about the coordination of
LLM activity at both the individual and collective level. At the individual level, a sin-
gle LLM may need to choose among multiple augmented-generation techniques or
select between different external memory stores. At the collective level, the activities
of several LLMs must be coordinated in pursuit of some larger information-process-

ing objective. While these challenges are important, they are not unique to the devel-
opment of LLM-based personal memory systems; they are, in fact, a standard feature
of contemporary efforts to implement multi-agent workflows. For individual LLMs,
current approaches to tool and memory-store selection include in-context learning
(e.g., Yao et al. 2023), function calling,7 and tool-learning techniques (e.g., Schick
et al. 2023). In-context learning is the most widely used approach. It involves giving
the model structured descriptions of available tools and the conditions under which
they should be invoked. Attempts to address the second challenge—the challenge
of coordinating multi-LLM activity—typically rely on agentic AI frameworks such
as LangChain and AutoGen. These frameworks provide mechanisms for structur-
ing inter-agent communication, delegating subtasks, and managing tool invocations,
often through forms of “conversational programming” that treat natural language as a
generic interface for agent control (see Wu et al. 2024). As these orchestration frame-
works continue to mature, the coordination demands posed by LLM-based personal
memory systems are likely to be absorbed into increasingly standardized design prac-
tices. Nevertheless, understanding how these orchestration techniques behave in the
context of personal memory applications remains an important focus area for future
research.


3 Memory Processes

Courtesy of their interpretive, generative, and dialogic capabilities, LLMs can par-
ticipate in multiple memory processes. In this section, we explore the role that LLMs
can play in four key processes, namely, encoding (the process of acquiring informa-
tion), elaboration (the process of enriching or elaborating on acquired information),
retrieval (the process of recalling information), and joint reminiscing (the process of
collaboratively re-visiting—and perhaps reconstructing—the past). Our aim here is
not to outline the architecture or functionality of a particular memory system; rather,
we focus on the contributions that LLMs can make to different memory processes.
For the most part, we keep the discussion grounded in the current state-of-the-art,
linking the proposed capabilities to the functionality of existing systems.

3.1 Encoding

Encoding refers to the initial capture of information concerning an individual’s
activities and lived experience. This process is, of course, supported by conventional
lifelogging technologies, such as fitness trackers, smartwatches, body-worn cameras,
medical sensors, and so on.8 But while lifelogging technologies can capture continu-
ous streams of physiological and behavioral data, LLMs are ideally placed to inter-
pret and annotate this data. An image received from a wearable camera, for example,
could be annotated with a linguistic description: “A peaceful solo canoeing trip on a

   Multimodal LLMs thus provide an important opportunity to augment the opera-
tion of lifelogging devices, helping to transform low-level sensorimotor data into
semantically rich, temporally structured representations of lived experience. Perhaps
more importantly, however, LLMs provide an opportunity to move towards a more
active and collaborative approach to encoding. To help us see this, consider that some
of the information obtained from lifelogging devices may be incomplete or ambigu-
ous. Given an elevated heart rate, for example, it may be hard to determine whether
an individual is experiencing stress, anxiety, or excitement. Similarly, an individual’s
internal thoughts, motivations, and intentions often leave no trace in the incoming
data stream.
   To address these problems, an LLM can leverage its dialogic capabilities to actively
solicit information from the user. Given the ambiguity surrounding an elevated heart
rate, for example, an LLM might pose a question to the user: “How are you feeling
right now?”9 Similarly, when gaps in the incoming data stream are detected, an LLM
can attempt to fill this gap by issuing a request to the user. If, for example, a user is
talking about a particular object (“I bought this at the market today”), an LLM can
ask the user to take a picture of the object. In such cases, the LLM plays an active
role in shaping the encoding process, using targeted prompts to solicit information in
a manner that optimizes the prospects for future mnemonic support.
   The general idea here is the same as that seen in the earlier cases of RAG, KAG,
SAG, and so on (see Sect. 2.3). Just as an LLM can interact with informational and
technological resources by generating certain token sequences (e.g., queries, search
terms, and programmatic instructions), so it can interact with a human user by
capitalizing on its facility with natural language—its ability to generate linguistic
expressions that trigger some sort of response from a human individual. The main
difference here relates to the nature of the resource that is the target of the LLM’s
interaction (i.e., a human individual as opposed to a retrieval system, search engine,
or code interpreter). In general, however, we can understand the active shaping of
encoding processes in more or less the same way as we understand other types of
augmented generation: Just as a well-formed query can surface relevant information
from an external datastore, a well-formed question can surface otherwise inacces-
sible information from the user. In both cases, the goal (for the LLM) is to generate

    The upshot is a novel form of augmented generation that builds on those discussed

of another entity.

those of conventional lifelogging. That is to say, the goal of encoding is to preserve
information in a manner that supports later recall. But the advent of LLMs yields
a potentially richer (and more complicated) vision of the encoding process. Multi-
modal LLMs provide opportunities for the interpretation and annotation of personal
data, while the notion of HAG opens the door to a more interactive and collabora-

together to create a richer record of lived experience.

3.2 Elaboration

Encoding results in the storage of information within one or more personal data
repositories that function as the external memory for one or more LLMs. Elaboration
refers to the processes that enrich and restructure this information.11 This may involve
the addition of semantic or contextual information to existing memory records, the
removal of redundant content, and the consolidation of newly-acquired information
with existing memory records. Some insight into the nature of elaborative processes

   Elaboration also provides an important opportunity for the creation of narrative
artifacts—informational structures that weave personal data into coherent repre-
sentations of the past.13 This is especially relevant given the intimate links between
narrative and mnemonic processes in contemporary memory research. In particular,
research in psychology and philosophy suggests that autobiographical memory is not
merely a store of discrete episodes; it is the basis for constructing an ongoing life
story that situates the self within an evolving temporal arc (McAdams 2001; McAd-
ams and McLean 2013). Through processes of selection, organization, and inter-
pretation, individuals craft narratives that explain how they came to be the person
they are and where their lives might be heading. This autobiographical narrative is
widely regarded as a cornerstone of narrative identity—the internalized and evolving
story that provides a person with a sense of unity, purpose, and temporal coherence
(Schechtman 2005; McAdams 2001).
   Narrational capabilities thus represent an important focus area for research into
personal memory systems, and it is an area where LLMs are poised to outperform
their technological predecessors. Courtesy of their language generation capabilities,
LLMs can draw on the data stored in personal data repositories, yielding narratives
that are not just factual summaries, but meaningful, temporally structured personal
stories that resonate with human users (see Yunusov et al. 2024). In this sense, LLMs
are poised to play an increasingly important role in what has been dubbed narrative
niche construction (Heersmink 2020)—the process by which a variety of mnemonic
resources (including narrative artifacts) are created and pressed into cognitive ser-
vice. This is important when it comes to understanding the distinctive contribution
of LLMs to memory-related research. Whereas lifelogging technologies provide us
with a vision of data being recorded for a mnemonic purpose, LLMs provide us with
a vision in which disparate bodies of data are progressively assimilated into an evolv-
ing life story. It is in precisely this sense, we suggest, that LLMs mark a new era in the
history of memory technology—one that marks the shift from the mere recording or
‘logging’ of personal data (i.e., lifelogging) to a process of life narration.

   It is no doubt natural to assume that the narratives generated by LLMs will be of

possible for life events to be depicted in a visual form, perhaps as a series of images
rendered in a comic-book style format. Over time, these synthetic digital artifacts
may come to acquire the status of an evocative object—an object that is poised to
prompt the recollection of the past in a manner similar to more familiar examples
such as photographs, a child’s drawing, or a broken watch (see Heersmink 2022a).
   Narratives vary not only in form—such as text or image—but also in temporal
scope, ranging from brief, singular events to extended periods of time. Conway and
Pleydell-Pearce (2000) propose that autobiographical memory is organized in a hier-
archical manner, comprising three levels: lifetime periods (e.g., the years you lived in
London), general events (e.g., recurring experiences like Friday dinners at a favorite
restaurant), and specific episodes (e.g., a recent trip to Italy for a conference). In prin-
ciple, LLMs could generate narratives that mirror these hierarchical levels, presenting
users with a coherent sense of personal history while allowing for focused retrieval at
different scales of granularity. At the time of writing, however, it is unclear how well
LLMs perform at generating longer-term or more abstract narratives, such as those
associated with lifetime periods and general events. For the most part, extant systems
focus on more concrete and temporally-constrained periods, such as those associ-
ated with specific events (e.g., Li et al. 2025). In this sense, the hierarchical scheme
proposed by Conway and Pleydell-Pearce (2000) may prove useful when it comes to
informing the development of future LLM-based memory systems.

3.3 Retrieval

Retrieval is a core feature of memory technologies. If LLMs are to function as per-
sonal memory systems, they should not just support the encoding and storage of the
past, they should also support the recollection of the past. One way to understand
this capability is via the notion of just-in-time information retrieval, where informa-
tion is retrieved (and presented to a user) in accord with the present context (Rhodes
and Maes 2000). Existing LLM-based memory systems, such as Memoro, rely on a
mixed initiative retrieval scheme, where information can be retrieved in response to
explicit user instructions (e.g., “What was the name of that restaurant we visited last
week?”) or information about the present context (Zulfikar et al. 2024).
   LLMs can also participate in more interactive and collaborative forms of remem-
bering. One example is cross-cueing, which has been observed in human dyads (Har-
ris et al. 2014). Systems like Tip-pong exemplify this process by engaging users
in dialogic exchanges during tip-of-the-tongue states (Ha et al. 2023). When a user
struggles to retrieve a specific memory item, the system initiates a conversational
back-and-forth—a “ping pong” of semantic prompts and clarifications—until the elu-
sive memory is eventually surfaced. In this case, the LLM does not merely retrieve
previously encoded information; rather, it participates in an active cue-elaboration
process that combines user-provided cues with its own recollections.

3.4 Joint Reminiscing

Joint reminiscing refers to the process by which earlier memories are revisited (and
possibly revised) by LLMs and human users. The resources generated during elabo-
ration are one of the obvious targets for joint reminiscing (see Sect. 3.2). This is par-
ticularly true of narrative artifacts, which undoubtedly benefit from some degree of
checking and refinement by a human user. As an extension of these processes, users
may be involved in the co-production of new narrative artifacts. Examples include
life story books (Elfrink et al. 2018) and multimedia biographies (Smith et al. 2009),
both of which have been used in clinical settings to help individuals retain a sense of
identity in the wake of progressive cognitive decline.
    In addition to the collaborative production and editing of narrative artifacts, LLMs
are poised to influence memory in a manner similar to that observed in human social
contexts. A nice example of this comes in the form of MindTalker—a conversational
agent intended for individuals with early-stage dementia (Xygkou et al. 2024). Built
using OpenAI’s GPT-4 model, MindTalker is designed to participate in reminiscence
therapy via a user’s photo gallery. In particular, the application engages users in con-
versations about particular photos by asking open-ended questions such as “Who is
in this photo?” or “What do you remember about this day?” This resembles the sort
of interventions that are sometimes seen in the case of lifelogging devices, such as
SenseCam (see Dubourg et al. 2016). But while these earlier interventions relied on
the support of a human caregiver, MindTalker yields a vision in which this socially-
supportive role is now occupied by an LLM.
    The notion of an LLM occupying a social role points to a broader issue concerning
the status of LLMs as memory technologies. Specifically, it highlights how LLMs
blur the boundary between technologically- and socially-shaped routes to mnemonic
influence. We saw this in the case of Tip-pong, which emulates a process that has
traditionally been studied in human–human dyads (i.e., cross-cueing). More gener-
ally, by engaging users in conversations about past events or helping them explore the
emotional significance of prior experiences, LLMs are poised to recreate the kind of
mnemonic scaffolding typically provided by human interlocutors (see Hirst and Ech-
terhoff 2012). Just as conversational interactions with others can help to strengthen,
weaken, or modify memories of the past, the conversational capabilities of LLMs
may open the door to similar forms of (technological? social? ) influence. Addressing
these questions is an important area for future research. It expands the scope of exist-
ing work by acknowledging the potential of a technological entity to participate in
processes of conversational remembering—processes that have typically been con-
fined to the realm of human–human interaction.


4 Component or Companion?

We have discussed some of the ways in which LLMs can contribute to mnemonic
processes. But how does this vision of LLMs as a memory technology connect with
ongoing philosophical debates about the environmentally-situated nature of human
memory processes?

                                                                              13
                                                                              P. Smart et al.


    One possibility is that we should regard LLMs as the constituents (or components)
of extended cognitive circuits, as per claims regarding extended cognition and the
extended mind (Clark and Chalmers 1998; Clark 2010). Relative to this perspec-
tive, an LLM would qualify as part of the physical machinery by which certain sorts
of mnemonic capacity are realized. Heersmink (2022b), for example, explores how
external artifacts—such as diaries, photographs, digital archives, and lifelogging
devices—can play a constitutive role in autobiographical memory, functioning as
integral parts of remembering processes.
    As far as we can tell, there is no reason why LLMs could not qualify as the con-
stituents of extended memory systems. Insofar as we understand memory as a par-
ticular sort of capacity—e.g., a capacity to coordinate current thoughts and actions
with regard to previously encountered information—then it is at least plausible that
LLMs could feature as part of the physical machinery that sustains this capacity.
To be a little more precise, we see no reason why LLMs could not form part of the
mechanisms that realize memory processes, where a memory process corresponds to
the manifestation (or exercise) of a mnemonic capacity that is ascribed to a particular
individual (see Smart 2024). In such cases, an LLM would operate as a so-called AI
extender (Hernández-Orallo and Vold 2019)—an AI system that qualifies as one of
the constituents of an extended cognitive system or an extended mind.
    On the other hand, it is far from clear that every process in which an LLM is
involved ought to be understood as an extended cognitive process. Rather than
approach LLMs from an extended perspective, some situations may call for a more
embedded approach (Rupert 2004), one that emphasizes the merely causal (but not
constitutive) contribution of LLMs to human memory processes. Perhaps, for exam-
ple, an LLM may qualify as an AI extender when it comes to semantic memory, but
fail to qualify as an AI extender when it comes to episodic memory. Rather than
regard extended and embedded approaches as competing and mutually exclusive cat-
egories, it may be better to adopt a more ecumenical position, one that tailors claims
of extended or embedded cognition to the details of particular cases. This is likely to
be important given the different ways that LLMs can contribute to human memory
processes.
    A further complication stems from the status of LLMs as social (or, at any rate,
quasi-social) entities (see Clowes in press). Consider, for example, the OS-1 sys-
tem, which is an LLM-based memory system modelled after the fictional character
Samantha in the movie Her (Xu et al. 2024). In one sense, it is certainly true that
OS-1 provides opportunities for cognitive extension, but the main objective of the
development effort was not so much to engineer an extended memory system as it
was to create an artificial social companion.
    There is, of course, no reason to think that social entities could not form part of an
extended memory system. It has long been recognized that other human individuals
(e.g., a caregiver or intimate companion) could play a constitutive role in cognitive
processes, thereby giving rise to what is known as socially-extended cognition (see
Clark and Chalmers 1998). The limiting factor, here, is more the extent to which an

extra-organismic resource meets the conditions for cognitive extension14 than it is
the nature of the resource itself. As noted by Clark and Chalmers (1998), appeals
to socially-extended cognition are likely to be limited to certain situations, such as
those featuring an “unusually interdependent couple.” But these situations need not
be so unusual once our attention turns to LLMs. Unlike a human companion, an LLM
may be more-or-less constantly available and always ready to assist with a cognitive
or epistemic task. In this sense, then, LLMs (qua social companions) may be much
better suited to support socially-extended cognition than their human counterparts.
At the same time, of course, an LLM is, at root, a technological entity. Accordingly,
LLMs may yield a hybrid form of cognitive extension: one that papers over (or at
least blurs) the otherwise crisp conceptual distinction between social and technologi-
cal forms of mnemonic (and, more generally, cognitive) extension.


5 A ‘Good’ Memory?

A natural concern about the use of LLMs as personal memory technologies centers
on their tendency to generate factually incorrect responses—a phenomenon widely
referred to as hallucination. Insofar as personal memory systems aim to support the
reliable recall of past events, one might reasonably worry that such errors undermine
the suitability of LLMs for personal memory applications. One way to mitigate hal-
lucinations is to employ the augmented-generation techniques described in Sect. 2.
But while these techniques are effective at reducing errors, none offers a guaranteed
solution. In the case of RAG, for example, errors may arise either because incorrect
information is retrieved from the external store (retrieval failure) or because informa-
tion is not properly assimilated into the model’s output (generation failure). Zhang
and Zhang (2025) provide a useful overview of these failure modes together with a
survey of recent efforts to improve the accuracy and fidelity of RAG-based systems.
    While there has been progress towards mitigating hallucinations, it remains
unclear whether hallucinations can ever be fully eliminated. The problem is that
LLMs are, at root, generative systems: they do not merely retrieve information from
internal or external memory but use such information to generate new representations
(token sequences) via an inferential process. This naturally invites comparisons with
constructivist (or generative) approaches to human memory (Michaelian 2011). Con-
structivist theories hold that remembering involves the active reconstruction of past
events from fragmentary traces, background schemas, and current goals. Because
this process relies on inference rather than literal replay, errors and distortions are a
recurring—and perhaps unavoidable—aspect of human memory. As Bernstein and
Loftus (2009, p. 373) note, “In essence, all memory is false to some degree. Memory
is inherently a reconstructive process, whereby we piece together the past to form a
coherent narrative that becomes our autobiography.”

    A propensity for error and distortion thus appears to be a common feature of both
LLM architectures and human bio-memory systems.15 But while LLM hallucinations
are widely regarded as flaws or defects—and therefore as problems to be remedied—the
treatment of human memory errors is far more nuanced. This is especially true in the
case of autobiographical memory, where an expanded appreciation of the cognitive,
affective, and social functions served by remembering has prompted a more positive
view of mnemonic errors. As Bluck (2003, p. 113) notes, “levels and types of accuracy
need not always be regarded as memory ‘failures’ but are sometimes integral to a self-
memory system that serves a variety of meaningful ends of human activity.” Conway et
al. (2004) make a similar point in their discussion of the self‐memory system, arguing
that autobiographical remembering is governed by a dynamic tension between adaptive
correspondence (the accurate retention of previously encountered information) and self-
coherence (the maintenance of a meaningful and temporally integrated sense of self). In
many cases, they suggest, the system privileges coherence: thematic elaborations, rein-
terpretations, and selective omissions that help sustain a stable and intelligible personal
identity. Habermas and Bluck (2000) likewise emphasize that narrative identity emerges
not from literal fidelity to experience but from the selective integration and interpretive
reconstruction of autobiographical memories into a coherent life story. From this per-
spective, a propensity for error and distortion is not a defect in a system designed for
accurate recollection, but a naturally recurring—and often productive—feature of a sys-
tem geared toward supporting psychological continuity and future-oriented reasoning.
    All this, we suggest, complicates the normative interpretation of LLM halluci-
nations. If human autobiographical memory routinely sacrifices accuracy in favor
of coherence, continuity, and psychological well-being, then it is at least plausible
that some LLM hallucinations might play a similar role in the context of a personal
memory system. Support for this idea stems from work by Sui et al. (2024). They
suggest that a propensity for hallucination “mirror[s] a human propensity to utilize
increased narrativity as a cognitive resource for sense-making and communication”
(p. 14274). Hallucinations, Sui et al. (2024) suggest, are the result of LLMs striving
to generate plausible and coherent outputs, as opposed to strictly accurate outputs.
This emphasis on coherence at the expense of accuracy is clearly problematic when
the goal is to accurately recall the past, but it is not clear that every application of an
LLM-based memory system ought to be designed around this goal.
    Our aim here is not to underplay the importance of hallucinations in a personal memory
context; it is simply to note that we should not judge hallucinations to be problematic in
the absence of a clearer understanding of the role that error and distortion play in normal
human memory. If the sorts of errors and distortions we observe in normal human bio-
memory are understood to play a productive role in the psychological and social lives of
individuals, then it is, at best, unclear that technical efforts should be geared to the elimina-
tion of those errors and distortions. At the very least, we may need to adopt a more nuanced
approach to hallucinations—one that recognizes the hazards they pose for perfect recall
while also acknowledging their potential value in the co-creation of a coherent life story.

   Evidently, there is a need for further research in this area. Such research, however,

precision, but whether they help users navigate and narrate their lives in ways that
support cognitive, emotional, and social flourishing. This, we suggest, is the standard
by which a genuinely ‘good’ memory system ought to be judged.


6 Conclusion

LLMs fascinate because of their peculiar facility with language. Like us, they can
adapt their responses to linguistic instructions and sustain temporally extended con-
versational exchanges. Such forms of linguistic competence open the door to novel
modes of human–machine interaction, enabling us to engage with LLMs in ways
that often resemble our interactions with other people. These capabilities are particu-
larly significant in the context of memory applications. As demonstrated by a number
of recent systems, LLMs can function as personal memory technologies, retrieving
information in response to conversational or contextual cues to yield various sorts of
mnemonic support (Xu et al. 2024; Zhong et al. 2024; Zulfikar et al. 2024).
   In the present paper, we explored some additional ways in which a facility with
language could be used to support the development of personal memory systems.
Firstly, we discussed how language opens the door to a more interactive, participa-
tory approach to the encoding of information. Unlike conventional lifelogging tech-
nologies, which passively capture data, LLMs are able to shape the structure of the
encoding process by (e.g.) requesting additional or clarifying information from a
human user. The upshot is a novel form of augmented generation—what we dubbed
human-augmented generation. This extends the types of augmented generation that
have been discussed in the wider (technical) literature.
   We also explored the role of LLMs in generating narrative artifacts. This, we sug-
gested, marks a shift from the notion of lifelogging to life narration. Whereas earlier
technologies focused on the ‘logging’ or recording of personal data, LLMs provide
opportunities to generate narrative artifacts across a variety of temporal scales. In this
respect, LLMs are poised to play an important role in the process of narrative niche
construction (see Heersmink 2020).
   Finally, we examined the status of LLMs as technological and/or social entities.
While LLMs are, at root, technological entities, their proficiency with language
enables them to function in the manner of a human social companion. This poten-
tially blurs the distinction between socially-extended and technologically-extended
forms of memory. The practical significance of this should be clear: by recapitulat-
ing the dynamics of socially-distributed remembering, LLMs yield opportunities to
shape human memory in ways that resemble those observed in long-standing human
relationships. In addition to the practical considerations, however, LLMs raise inter-

esting questions about the nature of LLM-based memory systems. Are such systems
best understood as a technological system that helps us remember the past, or are
they best understood as something more akin to a social companion—a new kind of
intimate ‘other’ that is poised to provide a variety of types of cognitive, affective, and
social support? And who is that intimate ‘other’, exactly? Is the LLM distinct from
you, or is it more a part of you—part of the material fabric that makes you the sort
of person you take yourself to be? Perhaps, as Clark (2025, p. 3) suggests, such enti-
ties will come to be viewed as a ‘borderline you’—an entity that “learn[s] from your
projects and choices, and play[s] a role in sculpting your future projects and choices.”
In this sense, an LLM is not simply an entity that helps you navigate the past; it is also
the co-creator of a shared autobiography. A co-narrator, perhaps, of the story of you.
